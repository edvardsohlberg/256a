Reading Response: Toys

Sonic Lighter and Ocarina were the most memorable pairing for me. Sonic Lighter is a lighter that cannot light anything, making it useless, which Wang cheerfully admits (Wang, 70). Ocarina flips that around, because it is a toy which turns out to be a real, playable instrument (Wang, 75). Back to the means and ends idea from last week, Sonic Lighter is almost entirely on the ends side, a gag that exists for its own sake. The Ocarina steps in both directions, which makes it more interesting to me. Still, I feel like I would try out the Sonic Lighter first.

The ocarina is explained by principles 2.2 and 2.3, “design inside-out” and “function follows form.” The iPhone is about the size of an actual four-hole pendant ocarina, and it already had a microphone that you could blow into (79). The tool was somewhat hidden in the device itself. This made me see my HW2 visualizer idea from a different perspective. The laptop microphone is a constraint I have to deal with and the chapter makes a good point about how it is often better to embrace and work with constraints rather than struggle against them.

According to principle 2.4, designers should use the physicality of their tools and I appreciated Wang for not pretending the phone was an ocarina. This is just stated plainly as its claim: “this is an Ocarina” (Wang, 80). Being a pianist, I've noticed that the digital pianos that have always impressed me are those which provide immediate response. The keys are weighted and push back on the fingers at every press. Even if breath, tilt for vibrato and fingers on glass are just a few inputs, it’s evident that they are sufficient.

The globe section left the strongest impression on me. Listening to strangers play from another part of the world with the knowledge of their name and city (Wang, 82), one user, a soldier on duty in Iraq, noted that it was the most calming thing he came across (Wang, 86). Principle 2.6, technology should create calm, is connected with the idea of ubiquitous computing by Mark Weiser introduced earlier in the chapter. The difference between social media and the ocarina is that one is designed to create engagement, distress and distraction. The other is meant to be engaged with, and the feedback isn’t immediate unless you’re willing to put the effort in. That is how I understand principle 2.5 as well. The idea behind all this technology is a feeling that the experience has absolutely nothing to do with technology–and, most likely, for this very reason, Wang's 96-year-old grandmother plays it (Wang, 88).

Wang calls Ocarina an expressive toy, something with a low barrier to entry and real expressive range. For my sound visualizer, I'd like to aim for that too. Ideally it would make someone want to use it, find ways to use it that I didn’t even think of, and release their inhibitions while engaging with a (possibly useless) tool that I created.
